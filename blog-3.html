Two Different Elections

Why Turnout Collapses Between Westminster and the Town Hall

United Ballots Research · July 2026 · 6 minutes analysis
In the 2024 electoral cycle, the turnout for the general election significantly surpassed that of the local elections, with participation levels nearly doubling in comparison. The observed pattern has persisted over several decades, and it cannot be accounted for by the actual power available.

Participation levels in the 2024 English local elections are projected to be approximately 50% of the turnout observed during the general election in the same year.
Inquiries directed at the general populace in Britain regarding the timing of the forthcoming general election are likely to elicit a range of speculative responses. Inquire about the upcoming election schedule for their council, and the candid response for many individuals is a lack of awareness regarding this information.
During the 2024 general election, voter turnout among registered individuals reached 59.7%, indicating a significant level of civic engagement within the electorate. During the local elections conducted in the same year, voter turnout across English councils, mayoralties, and Police and Crime Commissioner contests was approximately 30%. This figure represents about half of the turnout observed during the general election in the same country within the same twelve-month period. This occurrence is indicative of a recurring pattern. The Electoral Commission's data regarding the 2022 English local elections indicates a voter turnout of 35.6%. In contrast, the turnout for the 2023 elections was recorded at 32.0%. The subsequent report on the May 2025 local elections highlights varying turnout rates: 34.3% for county council contests, 35.2% for unitary authorities, and a notably lower 30.8% for Combined Authority Mayoral elections. These mayoral positions are significant, as they often oversee transport and economic development budgets that amount to billions of pounds.

A structural gap should be recognized as a deliberate phenomenon rather than a mere coincidence.


The disparity is rooted in structural factors rather than being a mere incidental occurrence. The analysis conducted by the Electoral Commission indicates that voter turnout for county council elections has consistently remained low over the past fifty years. Furthermore, it has shown a gradual decline in participation when these elections are not synchronized with UK general elections. Turnout for Combined Authority Mayoral elections, a recent addition to England's devolved governance framework established in 2017, has not surpassed 30% in 50% of the 27 elections conducted to date. The highest turnout recorded in a mayoral contest occurred in Cambridgeshire and Peterborough in 2021, reaching 36.4%.

Case-study research demonstrates the pattern with a high degree of specificity. A comprehensive analysis of local by-elections in Brighton and Hove revealed voter turnout varying between 21.8% and 65% across four distinct wards in 2024. This is in contrast to the overall turnout of 40.85% during the city’s full council elections in 2023, and the turnout rates of 60% to 70.4% observed in the 2024 general election within the same locality. The research, derived from interviews and local surveys, has pinpointed two prevalent factors: a significant portion of residents lack awareness regarding the specific responsibilities of the local council and the timing of its elections within the electoral calendar. Furthermore, this knowledge deficit exacerbates an already diminished perception of the impact of local voting.

The argument regarding the stakes is not substantiated.


The second point warrants careful examination, as it fails to withstand the realities pertaining to the scope of authority exercised by English local government. Local councils are responsible for establishing council tax rates, managing adult social care and children's services, maintaining road infrastructure, licensing various premises, and overseeing planning decisions that significantly influence the physical landscape of high streets and housing estates nationwide. Additionally, combined authority mayors are increasingly entrusted with considerable budgets for transport and economic development initiatives. This document does not represent a concise overview. The disparity in turnout is not commensurate with the significance of the stakes involved; rather, it appears to operate inversely. A nationally significant contest for a single Member of Parliament's vote within a 650-member assembly attracts a substantially greater number of voters compared to elections for local councils or mayoralties, which have a more immediate and frequent impact on the daily lives of most residents.


The disparity in campaign and media attention allocated to the two tiers of election is a significant factor contributing to the observed differences. General elections are comprehensively reported, organized around televised debates featuring party leaders, and contested by national campaigns that allocate substantial financial resources, adhering to stringent statutory expenditure limits. Local elections are increasingly dependent on the limited resources of local parties, which are significantly smaller in scale. Additionally, the extent of local media coverage has diminished notably, correlating with the substantial decline in local newspaper circulation observed over the past twenty years.

The disparity is not consistent across various categories of local authority. Ipsos's historical analysis of local election turnout indicates that shire district turnout is approximately 36%, while unitary authority turnout stands at 31%. Furthermore, turnout in metropolitan boroughs, which encompass many of England's largest cities, is notably lower at just 26%. London has demonstrated a notable deviation from established trends during instances when a prominent mayoral election coincided with other ballots. An ICM poll conducted prior to a London Mayoral election indicated that 69% of participants expressed certainty or a reasonable likelihood of voting, reflecting a ten-point increase following a two-week period of heightened campaign focus. The observed variation in turnout, occurring within a singular urban environment over a two-week period, substantiates the subsequent argument: voter engagement is significantly influenced by the salience and competitiveness of the electoral context, rather than being solely determined by a static level of civic interest within the locality.

The introduction of Police and Crime Commissioner elections in 2012 exemplifies the extreme manifestation of this dynamic. The inaugural PCC elections demonstrated a turnout of approximately 15% across England and Wales, marking one of the lowest participation rates ever observed for a nationwide poll in Britain. This is particularly notable given that PCCs are tasked with the direct responsibility of establishing police budgets and priorities within their respective force areas. A contest characterized by a distinct and compelling mandate garnered the participation of only one in seven voters. This low turnout can be attributed to the electorate's limited understanding of the role and responsibilities of a PCC, as well as the significance of the electoral choice. This situation underscores that the turnout disparity identified is primarily a result of deficiencies in communication and visibility, rather than any other factors.


The implications of the data are significant and warrant careful analysis. Understanding the underlying trends and patterns is essential for informed decision-making and strategic planning. A comprehensive evaluation will provide insights that can guide future actions and policies. The disparity in voter turnout between Westminster and local councils reflects the levels of visibility and perceived importance, rather than actual authority. Local councils and mayors wield control over budgets and services that have a more immediate impact on daily life than the majority of votes cast in Westminster. Based on the available evidence, it appears that addressing this gap primarily constitutes a communications challenge prior to any other considerations.

None of this is predetermined. In instances where local voter turnout has significantly increased, it has often coincided with the presence of a prominent local contest, such as a competitive mayoral election or a referendum-related issue. This trend indicates that the disparity in turnout is more closely related to the visibility of the matters at hand and the entities advocating for voter participation, rather than a consistent public apathy towards local governance. Ultimately, this represents a discourse centered on communication rather than indifference: the electorate that participates at nearly 60% in Westminster elections demonstrates its capacity to engage in local town hall elections as well. Based on the available evidence, there has been infrequent justification for such an equivalence.
