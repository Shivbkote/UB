Fifty Years in the Making

Mapping Diversity in the Modern House of Commons

United Ballots Research · July 2026 · 6 minutes analysis
The 2024 intake represents a significant milestone in the history of the UK Parliament, achieving unprecedented levels of diversity across two key metrics simultaneously. The extensive data series indicates significant advancements, while simultaneously highlighting the remaining gaps in achieving proportionality.

The proportion of Members of Parliament elected in 2024 who are women represents the highest level recorded in the history of the UK Parliament. Each newly convened Parliament serves as a significant data point within an extensive historical series that reflects the representatives chosen by Britain.

Each new Parliament serves as a significant data point within an extensive longitudinal series. The 2024 general election resulted in a notable achievement: the highest number and proportion of women elected to the House of Commons, alongside the highest recorded number of Members of Parliament from minority ethnic backgrounds. Both assertions are substantiated, credible, and warrant careful consideration. Neither element, when considered in isolation, provides a comprehensive understanding of the extent to which Westminster has evolved in terms of representation. Commence with the key metrics. As reported by the House of Commons Library, the election held on 4 July 2024 resulted in the election of 263 women, constituting 40% of the total 650 Members of Parliament. This marks an increase from the previous election in 2019, where 220 women were elected, representing 34% of the total MPs. It represents the ongoing progression of a protracted and gradual ascent. In 1979, the year that marked the election of Britain's first female Prime Minister, the representation of women in the House of Commons was notably limited, with only 19 women serving, constituting a mere 3% of the total membership. The percentage increased to 9% by 1992, subsequently doubling in 1997, when 120 women were elected. This marked the first instance in which women occupied more than ten percent of the seats. The data indicates a consistent upward trajectory in electoral participation, albeit with fluctuations, across successive elections: 18% in 2001, 22% in 2010, 29% in 2015, 34% in 2019, and culminating at 40% in 2024. Progress is both tangible and inconsistent.

The distribution of women MPs across parties exhibits significant variability, necessitating a nuanced approach rather than a generalized interpretation of "increased representation of women MPs" as a uniform, party-agnostic phenomenon. Post-2024 election analysis indicates that 46% of Labour MPs are women, in contrast to 24% of Conservative MPs and 44% of Liberal Democrat MPs, as reported by the Commons Library. In the 2024 election, 322 Members of Parliament (MPs) were elected for the first time, with women constituting 40% of this cohort. However, this percentage varied significantly among political parties, with newly elected Conservative MPs comprising 31% women, while newly elected Labour MPs reached 41%. This disparity underscores that the national average is influenced not only by the progress made within individual parties but also by the dynamics of seat gains and losses during the election cycle.

Representation concerning ethnicity conveys a narrative that is both authentic and inherently incomplete. No Member of Parliament representing a minority ethnic background was present in the House of Commons during the period from 1929 to 1987. In 1987, four individuals were elected, marking the initiation of a gradual yet consistent upward trajectory in electoral outcomes at each subsequent general election. As of 2019, approximately 66 Members of Parliament (MPs) identified as belonging to minority ethnic backgrounds. By 2024, this number increased to an estimated 90 MPs, representing roughly 14% of the House of Commons. This data is derived from research conducted by the House of Commons Library, which utilized estimates from the think tank British Future, as the Commons does not maintain official records on the ethnicity of its members, citing the subjective and personal nature of ethnic self-identification.


Document, and remain concise. In this context, the caveat assumes a critical role, transcending mere embellishment to become a fundamental component of the analysis. In the period of 2024/25, it is projected that approximately 16% of the population in the United Kingdom will originate from a minority ethnic background. If the House of Commons were to reflect that figure in a proportional manner, it would result in approximately 104 minority-ethnic Members of Parliament, which is about 14 more than the number currently elected. The record intake for 2024 represents a significant milestone in Westminster's approach to achieving demographic proportionality concerning ethnicity. However, it remains evident that there is still a gap to be addressed in this regard. Progress and a persistent gap represent complementary observations; they reflect the same phenomenon analyzed from two distinct perspectives.


A comparable dual interpretation is relevant to the representation of women. Forty percent represents a definitive record. Currently, the representation remains ten points below the 50% threshold that would accurately reflect the demographic composition of the UK adult population. Notably, it has taken over a century, from 1918, when women first acquired the right to stand for Parliament, until 2024, to achieve this level of progress. In summary, throughout the entire history of the House of Commons, a total of 693 women have been elected as Members of Parliament (MPs). This figure is currently surpassed by the number of male MPs in the existing cohort.

While gender and ethnicity represent significant dimensions for analyzing the evolving composition of Parliament, they are not the sole perspectives available. Notably, these two categories are distinguished by the availability of the most extensive continuous datasets. The 2024 election resulted in a significant turnover within the Parliament, with approximately 330 of the 650 Members of Parliament (MPs) being first-time representatives. This figure represents one of the highest proportions of new MPs observed in recent decades. A newer, significantly less comprehensive metric pertains to social class and educational background. In contrast to gender and ethnicity, there exists no official or semi-official tracking of Members of Parliament's socioeconomic origins that parallels the datasets analyzed herein. This absence has been highlighted by several political scientists as a critical shortcoming, resulting in an incomplete understanding of the representativeness of Parliament. This is particularly notable given that the two metrics that are systematically monitored continue to exhibit a consistent trend.

The implications of the data are significant and warrant careful analysis. Understanding the underlying trends and patterns is essential for informed decision-making and strategic planning. A comprehensive interpretation of the data will facilitate the identification of opportunities and challenges that may arise in the relevant context. Representation has experienced significant shifts; however, these changes have not been uniform across political parties. Despite record numbers, there remains a notable shortfall in achieving demographic proportionality concerning both gender and ethnicity. For parties and selection panels, the data indicates the necessity for sustained and intentional efforts, rather than relying on the assumption that the current trend will naturally reach completion without intervention.

It is important to note that the information presented does not advocate for any specific electoral reform, candidate-selection policy, or party strategy. There exists a significant divergence of opinion among reasonable individuals regarding the most effective mechanisms, ranging from all-women shortlists to comprehensive changes in boundaries and voting systems. The data provided does not serve to resolve these differing perspectives. The data clearly delineate the trajectory of representation within the Commons, indicating a gradual yet consistent shift towards aligning with the demographic profile of the nation. This evolution has been predominantly influenced by changes within two specific parties, rather than being uniformly distributed across the entire political spectrum. Furthermore, despite the unprecedented intake anticipated for 2024, it remains insufficient to achieve full proportionality based on the metrics analyzed in this report.


The disparity in campaign and media attention allocated to the two tiers of election is a significant factor contributing to the observed differences. General elections are comprehensively reported, organized around televised debates featuring party leaders, and contested by national campaigns that allocate substantial financial resources, adhering to stringent statutory expenditure limits. Local elections are increasingly dependent on the limited resources of local parties, which are significantly smaller in scale. Additionally, the extent of local media coverage has diminished notably, correlating with the substantial decline in local newspaper circulation observed over the past twenty years.

The disparity is not consistent across various categories of local authority. Ipsos's historical analysis of local election turnout indicates that shire district turnout is approximately 36%, while unitary authority turnout stands at 31%. Furthermore, turnout in metropolitan boroughs, which encompass many of England's largest cities, is notably lower at just 26%. London has demonstrated a notable deviation from established trends during instances when a prominent mayoral election coincided with other ballots. An ICM poll conducted prior to a London Mayoral election indicated that 69% of participants expressed certainty or a reasonable likelihood of voting, reflecting a ten-point increase following a two-week period of heightened campaign focus. The observed variation in turnout, occurring within a singular urban environment over a two-week period, substantiates the subsequent argument: voter engagement is significantly influenced by the salience and competitiveness of the electoral context, rather than being solely determined by a static level of civic interest within the locality.

The introduction of Police and Crime Commissioner elections in 2012 exemplifies the extreme manifestation of this dynamic. The inaugural PCC elections demonstrated a turnout of approximately 15% across England and Wales, marking one of the lowest participation rates ever observed for a nationwide poll in Britain. This is particularly notable given that PCCs are tasked with the direct responsibility of establishing police budgets and priorities within their respective force areas. A contest characterized by a distinct and compelling mandate garnered the participation of only one in seven voters. This low turnout can be attributed to the electorate's limited understanding of the role and responsibilities of a PCC, as well as the significance of the electoral choice. This situation underscores that the turnout disparity identified is primarily a result of deficiencies in communication and visibility, rather than any other factors.


The implications of the data are significant and warrant careful analysis. Understanding the underlying trends and patterns is essential for informed decision-making and strategic planning. A comprehensive evaluation will provide insights that can guide future actions and policies. The disparity in voter turnout between Westminster and local councils reflects the levels of visibility and perceived importance, rather than actual authority. Local councils and mayors wield control over budgets and services that have a more immediate impact on daily life than the majority of votes cast in Westminster. Based on the available evidence, it appears that addressing this gap primarily constitutes a communications challenge prior to any other considerations.

None of this is predetermined. In instances where local voter turnout has significantly increased, it has often coincided with the presence of a prominent local contest, such as a competitive mayoral election or a referendum-related issue. This trend indicates that the disparity in turnout is more closely related to the visibility of the matters at hand and the entities advocating for voter participation, rather than a consistent public apathy towards local governance. Ultimately, this represents a discourse centered on communication rather than indifference: the electorate that participates at nearly 60% in Westminster elections demonstrates its capacity to engage in local town hall elections as well. Based on the available evidence, there has been infrequent justification for such an equivalence.
